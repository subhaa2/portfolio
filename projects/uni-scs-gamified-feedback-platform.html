SCS Gamified Feedback Platform

A Year 2, two-trimester Professional Software Development client project that partnered with Singapore Cancer Society to turn event feedback into a mobile-responsive, RPG-inspired web platform with branching survey flows, role-based admin tooling, analytics, and final handover documentation.

Final Demo

Embedded final project walkthrough showing the participant-facing feedback flow, the admin side, and the finished delivery state after the customer-day iterations.

This project was delivered for Singapore Cancer Society as a client-facing feedback platform for events and programmes. The core brief was not just to digitise a form, but to raise response rates and improve the quality of participant feedback by turning the feedback process into something more engaging and easier to complete on the spot.

Across the customer-day cycle, the product evolved from an early virtual-pet direction into a more focused RPG-style feedback journey. Participants moved through a branching narrative, selected paths that influenced the ending they received, and completed feedback in a way that felt more like a lightweight interactive experience than a conventional post-event survey. At the same time, the staff side matured into a role-based dashboard with event management, analytics, exports, and administrative controls.

By the final handover, the platform had become a maintainable multi-part system rather than only a prototype: separate dashboard and participant-facing applications, containerised deployment, Supabase-backed services, admin and superadmin access rules, multilingual support, event-level controls, and a documentation package for future maintenance. The finished result balanced gamification, accessibility, operational control, and real client delivery constraints.

The early scope explicitly framed the project around higher completion and richer feedback through gamified interaction.

Client brief

  • The main objective was to increase event feedback response rates by roughly 40 to 60 percent while also improving the depth and usefulness of what participants submitted.
  • The scope called for a mobile-responsive feedback experience, basic staff analytics, event access tools such as QR or direct links, user testing, and a deliverable the client could realistically continue using.
  • Discovery also surfaced demographic and accessibility concerns, including digital literacy, time tolerance for feedback, and the need to avoid making the process feel heavy or intrusive.

How the concept evolved

  • The project began with a virtual-pet direction, then pivoted after client feedback toward a more conversational and RPG-style interface inspired by lighter game flows.
  • By Customer Day 3 and Day 4, the team had refined the storyline, simplified parts of the participant flow, removed unnecessary personal-data collection elements, and aligned the UI more closely with client expectations.
  • The later iterations introduced the final branching narrative logic, where user choices shaped the ending they received, making the feedback experience feel more personalised and consequential.

Participant-facing experience

  • The participant side was designed as a mobile-friendly web flow rather than a traditional static survey, using story progression and lighter game cues to encourage completion.
  • Reports across the customer days describe event access through QR or direct event-entry tools, branching cell-based choices, and accessibility-minded decisions for older users and mixed digital comfort levels.
  • Later refinements added optional background music with mute control, official SCS branding, and multilingual support across Singapore’s four national languages.

Admin and analytics features

  • The staff dashboard evolved from a basic reporting view into a real-time admin interface connected directly to the live database.
  • By the later customer days, organisers could manage questions, configure event settings, separate multiple events cleanly, export CSV-friendly datasets, and track analytics without developer intervention.
  • Final dashboard enhancements included pie charts for sentiment-style breakdowns, a booth completion rate tracker, role-aware controls, and direct QR generation inside the dashboard.

Access control and operations

  • The project added a dedicated dashboard login and formal role-based access control so superadmins had full platform authority while admins were restricted to assigned events.
  • A superadmin console was added to assign event access, while privileged actions such as certain imports or deletions were hidden or blocked for lower roles.
  • Privacy and operational controls also improved over time through anonymous submission identifiers, row-level security in the database layer, and clearer separation between participant and staff functionality.

Architecture and deployment

  • The reports identify a stack built around Next.js, Tailwind CSS, Supabase, GitHub, and Render during the earlier delivery stages.
  • For final handover, the application was moved into a cloud-native containerised setup coordinated by Docker Compose, with separate containers for the dashboard, feedback form, and backend services.
  • The backend responsibilities were split into event, feedback, and analytics services, improving maintainability and making the system easier to reason about as a real delivered product rather than a one-off demo.

Backend service design

  • The Events Service handled event creation, quest management, and admin assignment while validating tokens and roles before operations were allowed.
  • The Feedback Service stored responses and associated answers through a single RPC-backed operation so the system would not leave partial or inconsistent feedback records behind.
  • The Analytics Service computed engagement metrics such as completion rates, skipped-question frequency, and recent response trends on the server side to reduce load on the participant flow.

Why it mattered

  • This was not just a styling exercise around surveys; it was a client project about improving real organisational feedback collection through interaction design, systems thinking, and maintainable delivery.
  • The team had to respond to customer feedback repeatedly, pivot the concept, refine the information architecture, and turn a fun-facing idea into something operationally useful for staff.
  • The final result demonstrated how gamification, accessibility, analytics, RBAC, and deployment concerns can all coexist in one end-to-end product when a project is treated as a real handover rather than only a coursework prototype.
