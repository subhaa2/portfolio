Scenario-Based Fire Preparedness Game

A Year 2, Trimester 1, 2025 Human Computer Interaction team project that improved a fire-safety learning game through iterative UI redesign, usability testing, clearer action labels, stronger visual contrast, and sound-based feedback.

Scenario-selection slide from the presentation showing the two learning contexts used in the project: a kitchen fire and a car fire.

This Human Computer Interaction project focused on a scenario-based fire preparedness game designed to improve emergency readiness through interactive learning rather than passive reading. The game placed users inside realistic situations, especially kitchen-fire and car-fire scenarios, where they had to make fast decisions in a controlled environment.

The design direction was intentionally simple and interactive, but the team did not stop at visual polish. The final report documents a full iteration cycle across menu labels, locked-state feedback, in-game instructions, navigation state, colour contrast, touch-target sizing, text readability, and audio feedback. That made the project less about a one-off prototype and more about showing how HCI decisions can be tested and improved.

Two focused experiments anchored the final evaluation. The first tested whether changing the shortcut label from Scenario Info to Quick Start improved clarity. The second tested whether adding button sound effects improved perceived responsiveness and satisfaction. Both experiments showed statistically significant improvements, which gave the redesign concrete evidence rather than relying only on opinion.

Project concept

  • Designed an interactive learning game to improve fire-emergency preparedness through realistic decision-making scenarios rather than static instruction.
  • Used kitchen-fire and car-fire situations so users could practise identifying risks, choosing the right action, and understanding consequences inside a safer simulated context.
  • Followed a simple, guided UI approach so the learning value came from clear interaction rather than from complex controls.

Change record highlights

  • Renamed the ambiguous Scenario Info button to Quick Start so returning users could reach their last-played scenario more intuitively.
  • Added feedback for locked scenarios, including a popup explaining why a scenario was unavailable and how to unlock it.
  • Improved usability through an in-game help button, active-tab highlighting, larger settings controls, larger statistics text, and stronger colour contrast across the main screens.
  • Extended the feedback layer with button SFX, scenario audio, and win/lose sounds so interactions felt more responsive and less silent.

Experiment 1 results

  • Tested the clarity of the shortcut label using a between-subject design with 5 participants per condition.
  • When the button was labelled Scenario Info, 0 out of 5 participants used it; with Quick Start, 5 out of 5 participants used it correctly.
  • Mean completion time improved from 14.89s to 5.20s, mean misclicks dropped from 4.20 to 0.00, and mean clarity rating rose from 1.2 to 4.8.
  • The report cites statistically significant results using Fisher's exact test and Mann-Whitney U, supporting both the objective and the hypothesis for label clarity.

Experiment 2 results

  • Tested whether button sound effects improved satisfaction with interface feedback using another between-subject setup with 5 participants per condition.
  • The sound-enabled version achieved a mean satisfaction rating of 4.67, compared with 3.00 for the silent version.
  • The report records a statistically significant Mann-Whitney U result at p ≈ 0.0098, supporting the claim that audio cues improved perceived responsiveness.
  • This made the project a good example of how small interaction details, such as feedback timing and sound, can materially change user perception.

HCI takeaways

  • Action-oriented wording reduced hesitation and made the interface easier to understand without additional explanation.
  • Feedback design was not limited to visuals; sound cues, clearer lock states, and accessible contrast all contributed to perceived responsiveness.
  • The project shows a practical HCI workflow: identify usability friction, redesign the interface, then validate the change with a structured experiment.
